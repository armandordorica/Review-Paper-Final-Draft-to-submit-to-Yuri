Apologies again for the delay. I received one review and looked at the paper by myself.

The paper has been substantially improved in this revision, and it has strong potential to provide a helpful reference point for readers.

Beside the aspects mentioned by the reviewer, a number of minor and not-so-minor issues however have to be resolved.

Minor:
References need polishing.
- Please use upper-case and lower-case notations consistently
- Replace references to preprints with references to published papers
- Kang & McAuley 2018 is repeated
- Use numbered references in the text
- "Wikipedia contributors." -> Please find a better reference to CPM

Make sure all abbreviations are properly introduced, including SL in the introduction

Not-so-minor:

Some DOIs in the references are pointing to the wrong papers (which I assume is a result of AI use, which should be declared in the paper), e.g. Mehrotra et al, 2018, Wen et al., 2019, Zhao et al., 2020, and others

Check which papers are referenced for which claim throughout. This issue from the first version seems to persist to some extent, e.g., "Rather than replacing SL, RL can be viewed as embedding supervised prediction within a sequential control framework [Chen
et al. 2022]." -> Not sure where this is indicated in the provided reference; the technical definition itself should be double-checked and potentially revised as well. Maybe avoid the term "supervised prediction" here.

The introduction says: "Second, RL leverages off-policy estimators that re-weight observed trajectories
under a candidate policy to evaluate counterfactual trajectories from logged data ..." I don't think this is generally true, but maybe only for specific RL implementations. Please check.

The statement "First, SL ignores policy-induced distribution shifts" is maybe also too strong in general.

In Section 3, the paper somehow suggests a progression from heuristics to supervised ML to deep learning to bandits and RL. While the paper states (close to Fig 1) that things happen in parallel, it also somewhere says that RL is the next step to ad modeling. Please double-check.

The summary of the blended reward /utility of Zhao et al. 2020 in Table 6 (last column) should be checked; the formulas shown here should be close to what is reported in the original paper. Please check if any confusion between Zhao et al 2020 and Zhao et al 2021 maybe have happened as well.

The LinkedIn system (Yan et al., 2020), which is referred to multiple times in the text, does not seem to be an RL-based system. While the text mentions that is based on constrained optimization in Table 6, it is a bit unclear why the system is discussed in Table 6 in the first place, given that the paper is about RL-based systems. A clarification seems necessary.

Reviewer: 1

Recommendation: Accept

Comments:
Thanks for this much improved second draft, which I recommend for acceptance.

Your paper is now a useful, accessible primer on the important topic of joint optimisation of advertisements and organic content. I found the paper enjoyable to read, with clear signposts and taxonomies guiding the reader through the literature. The discussion of trade-offs and the grounding of theory with experimental results was another strength.

The only real stumbling point was Section 4.4. Here, the taxonomy of state space representation could be used better. I agree with separating Markovianity and Expressiveness as different concepts, they end up yielding very similar values that don't really illuminate the reader at all.  PinnerFormer's Markovianity explained as "Fixed-size embedding summarizing multi-week engagement trajectory" vs Expressivity described as "single user embedding capturing long-term preference structure". TransAct "Long-term embedding combined with real-time session sequence" vs "Session sequence plus long-term embedding covering both horizons". This would be pretty straightforward to fix. Markovianity on how well the past history is compressed into the representation for predicting next states, e.g. PinnerFormer could say something about how "self-attention over multi-week sequences renders the transition probabilities conditionally independent" and Expressivity on missing information, like "pure user-side that misses page-level contextual features" (if that's true!).
The section would also benefit from a crisper contrast between SlateQ's immediate "slate-so-far" state and PinnerFormer's long-term user history state. I think this was somewhat buried. Expanding the discussion and making this more explicit would help the reader to grasp the practical differences and the wide choice possible in this domain.
